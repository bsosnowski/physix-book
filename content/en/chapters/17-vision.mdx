# The Eye & Vision

The eye is chapter 16's thin lens, built from protein: a cornea-and-lens
system that projects a real, inverted, diminished image onto the retina
— about 2 cm behind the lens. Your brain flips it back and calls it
"the world".

## The eye as an instrument

Unlike a camera (which moves its lens), the eye keeps the image
distance **fixed** — the eyeball's length — and changes the lens
**power** instead. Ciliary muscles squeeze the lens fatter for near
objects: **accommodation**.

$$
P_{needed} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{L} \quad \text{(diopters, metres)}
$$

Reading at 25 cm demands about 4 D more than gazing at the horizon.
A young lens can supply +10 D of accommodation; by age 50 it stiffens
toward zero — **presbyopia**, the reason reading glasses appear in
every middle-aged pocket.

**Experiments to try**

1. **Normal, distant**: rays converge exactly on the retina — the
   *Sharp* readout says 1. Move the object closer without touching the
   power: the focus slips behind the retina, the blur circle grows.
2. Now raise the eye power until sharp again — that is accommodation.
   Compare with the *Power needed* readout.
3. **Reading** preset shows the accommodated state. **Presbyopia**
   shows the same near object with a stiff lens: add +3 D of glasses
   and watch the blur collapse.

## When focus fails

Two geometric mismatches, two opposite fixes:

- **Myopia** (short-sighted): eyeball too **long** — distant light
  focuses *in front of* the retina. Fix: **diverging** (negative)
  glasses, which pre-spread the rays so the eye's focus lands further
  back.
- **Hyperopia** (far-sighted): eyeball too **short** — focus would land
  *behind* the retina. Fix: **converging** (positive) glasses.

Because thin lenses in contact add powers,
$P_{total} = P_{eye} + P_{glasses}$ — the optometrist's whole job is
finding the number that makes the sum right.

**Experiments:** run **Myopia**, note the negative focus error; switch
to **Myopia + glasses** and watch the focus walk back onto the retina.
Repeat with the hyperopia pair. Then prescribe your own: set the
eyeball length to 2.5 cm and find the glasses power that zeroes the
error for distant objects.

<Exercise title="Prescribe the glasses">
A myopic eye is 2.4 cm long with relaxed power 50 D. What glasses power
focuses distant objects (u ≈ ∞) exactly on its retina?

<Solution>
Need $P_{total} = 1/L = 1/0.024 \approx 41.7$ D for parallel rays.
$P_{glasses} = 41.7 - 50 = -8.3$ D — a strong minus lens. (Real
prescriptions are smaller because the cornea does most of the eye's
~60 D; our reduced-eye model folds it all into one lens.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why squinting helps">
Before glasses, people squint to see far details. Using the blur-circle
idea, explain why a narrow eyelid slit sharpens a defocused image.

<Solution>
Blur circle diameter ≈ pupil size × |focus error| / image distance.
Squinting shrinks the effective aperture, so the cone of rays reaching
the retina narrows — the blur circle shrinks proportionally even though
the focus error is unchanged. Same trick as a pinhole camera: trade
brightness for depth of field.
</Solution>
</Exercise>
