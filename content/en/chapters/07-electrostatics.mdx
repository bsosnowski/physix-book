# Electric Charge & Field

Rub a balloon on your hair and it sticks to the wall. No glue, no
magnetism — **electric charge**, the third fundamental actor of this
book after mass and… well, mass again. Charge comes in two signs, and
unlike masses, like charges *repel*.

## Charging bodies

Charge is never created — only moved. Three ways to move it:

- **Friction (triboelectric):** rubbing transfers electrons between
  materials. Glass loses them (becomes +), rubber gains them (−).
- **Contact:** touching a charged body shares its excess charge.
- **Induction:** a nearby charge *rearranges* the charges in a neutral
  body without touching it — the near side becomes opposite, and that
  is why the balloon sticks to a neutral wall.

The total charge of an isolated system is conserved, always.

## Conductors and insulators

In **conductors** (metals, salty water, you) some electrons roam freely;
in **insulators** (glass, plastic, dry air) every electron is locked to
its atom. Consequences:

- Excess charge on a conductor spreads to its **surface** and the field
  *inside* is zero — the charges rearrange until they cancel it.
- On an insulator, charge stays exactly where you put it (that balloon
  patch).
- A neutral insulator still responds: its molecules **polarize**,
  shifting charge slightly within each molecule.

## Coulomb's law and the field

Two point charges push or pull along the line joining them:

$$
F = k\,\frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}, \qquad k \approx 9\times10^9\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2
$$

Same inverse-square shape as gravity — but vastly stronger, and with
two signs. Instead of forces-at-a-distance we picture a **field**:
$\vec{E} = \vec{F}/q$, a vector at every point telling a positive test
charge which way to go. Field lines start on + and end on −; their
density is the field's strength.

**Experiments to try**

1. **Dipole**: drag the charges apart and together — watch the field
   lines stretch. The line pattern always leaves + and enters −.
2. **Like charges**: find the point where the small grid arrows vanish
   (the field cancels). Is it exactly midway? Now make the charges
   unequal — where does the null point move?
3. Set <M v="q2">q_2</M> to 0: a single charge's radial field, perfect
   $1/r^2$. Release the **test charge** and watch it accelerate outward.
4. With the dipole, release the test charge: its curved trail is a
   field line *only because it starts at rest* — with initial velocity
   it would overshoot (inertia!).
5. Drag a negative charge close to the positive one and watch the test
   charge get absorbed — then respawn. That is a spark, in miniature.

<Exercise title="The neutral wall">
A negatively charged balloon sticks to an electrically neutral wall.
The wall has zero net charge — so why is there attraction?

<Solution>
Induction/polarization: the balloon's field pushes the wall's surface
electrons slightly away, leaving the near surface positively charged.
The attracted + side is *closer* than the repelled − side, and Coulomb
force grows with proximity — so attraction wins despite zero net charge.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Doubling distance">
Two charges attract with force F. You double both charges and double
their separation. What is the new force?

<Solution>
$F' = k(2q_1)(2q_2)/(2r)^2 = 4/4 \cdot F = F$ — unchanged. The two
doublings cancel exactly; a neat check that you trust the formula more
than your intuition.
</Solution>
</Exercise>
