# Voltage & Capacitance

Voltage is the *push per charge* — the reason current flows at all.
A bird on a 400 kV line is perfectly safe because both feet are at the
same potential: no difference, no push, no current.

## What voltage means

Moving charge $q$ through a potential difference $V$ exchanges energy

$$
W = qV \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad V = \frac{W}{q}
$$

One volt = one joule per coulomb. In a **uniform field** between parallel
plates the potential drops steadily across the gap:

$$
E = \frac{V}{d}
$$

Field lines point "downhill" in potential; equipotential surfaces cut
them at right angles. The electronvolt — the energy one electron gains
crossing 1 V — is the natural currency of atoms: chemistry happens at a
few eV, which is why a 1.5 V battery can run a clock but never an X-ray
tube.

**Experiments to try**

1. At fixed <M v="V">V</M>, double the gap <M v="d">d</M>: the field
   readout halves. The same push, spread over twice the distance.
2. Click between the plates to release a test charge. Watch it
   accelerate uniformly — constant field, constant force. Its gained
   energy readout climbs linearly with the distance crossed: $W = qEd$.
3. Insert the **ceramic dielectric**: capacitance jumps ×6, but E stays
   put — the battery pins V, and E = V/d doesn't care about the filling.

## The capacitor

Two plates, a gap, and stored energy:

$$
C = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r \frac{A}{d}, \qquad Q = CV, \qquad
U = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2
$$

Bigger plates, tighter gap, better dielectric → more capacitance.
Cameras dump a capacitor's energy into a flash in milliseconds; your
phone's touchscreen is a grid of tiny capacitors that your finger
detunes.

<Exercise title="Flash energy">
A camera flash capacitor is 100 µF charged to 300 V. How much energy
does the flash deliver? If it fires in 2 ms, what is the average power?

<Solution>
$U = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2 = 0.5 \times 10^{-4} \times 9\times10^4 = 4.5$ J.
Over 2 ms → $P = 2250$ W — a hundred times the wall socket's steady
draw, released in a blink. Capacitors are power amplifiers in time:
slow charge, fast dump.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Safe gap">
Air breaks down (sparks) at about 3 kV/mm. Your capacitor has a 0.5 mm
air gap. What is the maximum safe voltage?

<Solution>
$V_{max} = E_{breakdown} \times d = 3\ \text{kV/mm} \times 0.5\ \text{mm}
= 1.5$ kV. Halve the gap and you halve the safe voltage — the eternal
trade-off of capacitor design: capacitance wants small d, insulation
wants large d.
</Solution>
</Exercise>
