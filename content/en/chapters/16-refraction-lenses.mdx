# Refraction & Lenses

Light slows down in matter, and that single fact bends rivers of photons
into eyes, cameras and optical fibres.

## Snell's law

The index of refraction is $n = c/v$ — how much slower light travels in
the medium. Crossing a boundary, the wavefronts pivot so that

$$
n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2
$$

Entering a denser medium ($n_2 > n_1$) bends the ray **toward** the
normal; leaving it bends away. Drag the incident ray below, or use the
<M v="theta">\theta_1</M> slider:

Notice the faint reflected ray — some light always bounces (about 4% at
an air–glass surface, per the *Reflected* readout). The brighter the
reflection, the dimmer the refraction: energy is conserved.

## Total internal reflection

Going from dense to rare ($n_1 > n_2$), the refracted ray bends away from
the normal — until it grazes the surface at the **critical angle**:

$$
\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}
$$

Beyond $\theta_c$ there is no refracted ray at all: **100% reflects**.
Water's critical angle is ≈ 48.8°, diamond's only ≈ 24.4° — light entering
a cut diamond bounces around inside before escaping, which is the
sparkle. Optical fibres are the same trick stretched into a kilometre.

**Experiments to try**

1. **Water → air** preset: raise the angle slowly and watch the
   refracted ray flatten, then vanish exactly at the *Critical angle*
   readout.
2. **Diamond**: even 20° is close to trapping light. Compare with glass.
3. Set $n_1 = n_2$: no bending at all — boundaries only matter when the
   speed changes.
4. Watch the *Reflected* percentage grow toward 100% as you approach
   the critical angle from below.

## Thin lenses

A lens is refraction engineered twice. For a thin lens of focal length
<M v="f">f</M>, object and image distances obey

$$
\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}, \qquad m = -\frac{v}{u}
$$

Positive $f$ — converging; negative — diverging. Opticians prefer
**power** in diopters: $P = 1/f$ (with $f$ in metres). The three
principal rays below always locate the image: parallel→focus,
through-center→straight, through-focus→parallel.

<Sim id="lens" />

**Experiments to try**

1. **Camera**: far object → real, inverted, diminished image just beyond
   F. That is literally what happens on your phone's sensor.
2. Drag the object toward F: the image races away and grows
   (**Projector** territory). At exactly F it escapes to infinity.
3. Cross inside F: the image flips to **virtual, upright, magnified** —
   you are holding a magnifying glass.
4. **Diverging lens**: whatever you do, the image stays virtual, upright
   and small. Why can't it ever catch an image on a screen?

<Exercise title="The disappearing image">
A candle sits 15 cm from a lens with f = 15 cm. Where is the image?
Explain what a screen placed anywhere behind the lens would show.

<Solution>
$1/v = 1/f - 1/u = 0$ → $v = \infty$: the rays exit parallel and never
meet. No screen position shows a focused image — only a blur of constant
size. This is how a collimator (searchlight) works: put the bulb at F to
get a parallel beam.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Fibre optics budget">
A glass fibre core has n = 1.50, cladding n = 1.40. Find the critical
angle at the core–cladding boundary. Why must the cladding exist at all?

<Solution>
$\theta_c = \arcsin(1.40/1.50) \approx 69.0°$ — rays hitting the wall
more grazing than that stay trapped. Without cladding, scratches,
fingerprints or water on the bare core would change $n_2$ locally and
leak light; the cladding keeps the boundary pristine and controlled.
</Solution>
</Exercise>
