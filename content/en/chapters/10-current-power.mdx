# Work & Power of Current

Current does work: it spins motors, lights rooms and — inevitably —
heats things up. The bookkeeping is one equation deep.

## Work and power

Moving charge $\Delta Q$ through a potential difference $V$ does work
$W = V\,\Delta Q$. Divide by time:

$$
P = \frac{W}{t} = V I
$$

Combine with Ohm's law and two more forms appear:

$$
P = VI = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}
$$

In a resistor **all** of it becomes heat (Joule's law):
$Q = I^2 R t$. This is a bug in a transmission line and a feature in a
kettle.

**Experiments to try**

1. **Perfectly insulated** preset: heating is a straight line on the
   temperature graph. Verify the slope against $P/(mc)$.
2. Halve the voltage: the slope drops **fourfold** ($P \propto V^2$).
   Confirm on the graph.
3. **Real kettle**: about 2 kW into 1 kg of water. Predict the time to
   boil from $Q = mc\Delta T$, then watch (remember the 60× time-lapse).
4. **Leaky pot**: with losses, temperature levels off where
   $P = \kappa\,(T - T_0)$. Find that equilibrium temperature.
5. Once boiling starts, energy keeps flowing but temperature stops at
   100 °C. Where does the energy go now?

## The kilowatt-hour

Utilities bill energy, not power. One kilowatt-hour is a kilowatt drawn
for an hour:

$$
1\ \text{kWh} = 1000\ \text{W} \times 3600\ \text{s} = 3.6\ \text{MJ}
$$

The simulator's cost readout uses an illustrative 0.8 per kWh — your
actual tariff goes here.

<Exercise title="Cost of a cup of tea">
How much does it cost to heat 0.25 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C in a
perfectly efficient kettle, at 0.8 per kWh?

<Solution>
$Q = mc\Delta T = 0.25 \times 4186 \times 80 \approx 83.7$ kJ
$= 0.0233$ kWh → cost ≈ **0.019** — under two groszy/forint/cents.
Heating water is cheap; *keeping* it hot all day is what costs money
(losses run 24/7).
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why transmission lines use high voltage">
A line of resistance $R$ delivers power $P$ at voltage $V$. Show that
the line's own heating loss scales as $1/V^2$ — and why grids use
hundreds of kilovolts.

<Solution>
Delivering $P$ at $V$ requires $I = P/V$. Line loss is
$I^2R = P^2R/V^2$. Ten times the voltage → hundred times less loss.
Transformers make the voltage trade cheap for AC — the core reason
Tesla's AC beat Edison's low-voltage DC grid.
</Solution>
</Exercise>
