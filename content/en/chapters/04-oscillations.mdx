# Oscillations

Anything that sits in a stable equilibrium and gets nudged will oscillate.
That is why the same mathematics describes springs, pendulums, circuits
and molecules.

## The harmonic oscillator

A spring pulls back proportionally to displacement (Hooke's law), so

$$
m\ddot{x} = -kx
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x(t) = x_0 \cos(\omega t), \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
$$

The period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ depends on the hardware, **not** on the
amplitude — pull it twice as far, it still ticks at the same rate.

**Experiments:** double the mass — by what factor does the period grow?
Watch the *phase portrait* (velocity vs displacement): undamped motion
traces a closed ellipse, because energy is conserved.

## The pendulum

For a pendulum of length <M v="L">L</M> released from
<M v="theta0">\theta_0</M>, the exact equation is

$$
\ddot{\theta} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta
$$

The famous formula $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ comes from replacing
$\sin\theta \approx \theta$ — valid only for small swings. At large
amplitudes the true period is *longer*:

$$
T = T_0 \cdot \frac{1}{\mathrm{AGM}\!\left(1, \cos\frac{\theta_0}{2}\right)}
$$

**Experiments to try**

1. **Small swing** ($15^\circ$): exact and approximation agree almost
   perfectly. Compare both period readouts.
2. **Large swing** ($120^\circ$): the ghost runs ahead — the real pendulum
   is slower. How much longer is the exact period? (Read both values.)
3. Change **length** mid-swing: the pendulum instantly swings faster or
   slower — $T \propto \sqrt{L}$.
4. Take it to the **Moon**: by what factor does the period grow?

## Damping

Real oscillators lose energy. Adding viscous drag $-c\dot{x}$:

$$
m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = 0
$$

The behaviour depends on the **damping ratio**
$\zeta = c / 2\sqrt{km}$:

- $\zeta < 1$ — **underdamped**: oscillates inside a decaying envelope,
- $\zeta = 1$ — **critically damped**: returns to equilibrium as fast as
  possible without overshooting (car suspensions, door closers),
- $\zeta > 1$ — **overdamped**: crawls back slowly.
