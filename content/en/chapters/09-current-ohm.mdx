# Current, Resistance & Ohm's Law

Static charge is a stored promise; **current** is that promise being kept.
Connect a battery to a wire and the electric field inside pushes free
electrons into a slow, steady drift.

## What is current?

Current is charge per unit time through a cross-section:

$$
I = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} \qquad [I] = \text{A} = \text{C/s}
$$

Surprise: individual electrons drift at fractions of a millimetre per
second, yet the lamp lights instantly — the *field* propagates near light
speed and sets **all** electrons moving at once, like water in a full pipe.

## Resistance and Ohm's law

Collisions with the lattice impede the drift. For a uniform wire:

$$
R = \rho\,\frac{L}{A}
$$

— longer means more resistance, thicker means less, and $\rho$ is the
material's resistivity. For ohmic conductors, current is proportional to
voltage:

$$
I = \frac{V}{R}
$$

Double <M v="V">V</M> and the carriers below double their pace. Double
<M v="R1">R_1</M> and they slow down. That proportionality *is* Ohm's law.

## Series and parallel

**Series** — one path. The same current flows through everything, and
the voltage drops add up (Kirchhoff's voltage law):

$$
R_{series} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3, \qquad V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3
$$

**Parallel** — several paths. Each branch sees the full voltage, and the
currents add (Kirchhoff's current law):

$$
\frac{1}{R_{parallel}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}
$$

The total parallel resistance is always *smaller than the smallest
branch* — more lanes on the motorway.

**Experiments to try**

1. Start in **Series**: verify the three voltage readouts add up to the
   battery voltage, and that the biggest resistor takes the biggest drop.
2. Flip the topology slider to **Parallel**: now every branch reads the
   full 12 V, but currents differ. Check $I_{total} = I_1+I_2+I_3$.
3. **Three equal** in parallel: total resistance should be exactly
   $R/3$. Verify against the readout.
4. Try **Near short circuit**: tiny resistance, huge current — watch the
   carriers race. Why do fuses exist?
5. Change voltage *live* while watching the current–time graph: a
   straight proportionality, step by step.

<Exercise title="Christmas lights">
Twenty identical lamps in series share 230 V. One lamp's filament breaks.
What happens to the others — and how must they be wired so one failure
doesn't kill the whole string?

<Solution>
In series the broken filament opens the only path: current stops
everywhere, all lamps go out ($I = 0$). Wiring lamps in parallel gives
each its own path across 230 V — one failure leaves the rest untouched.
That is why household outlets are parallel circuits.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Design a voltage divider">
You have a 12 V battery and need 4 V across $R_2$. Choose $R_1$ and
$R_2$ in series (ignore $R_3$ by setting it to 1 Ω — small enough to
neglect, or account for it exactly).

<Solution>
Series drops are proportional to resistance: $V_2 = V\,R_2/(R_1+R_2+R_3)$.
With $R_3 = 1\,\Omega$: pick $R_2 = 20\,\Omega$, then
$R_1 = R_2(V/V_2 - 1) - R_3 = 20 \cdot 2 - 1 = 39\,\Omega$. Check:
$12 \cdot 20/60 = 4$ V. ✓
</Solution>
</Exercise>
