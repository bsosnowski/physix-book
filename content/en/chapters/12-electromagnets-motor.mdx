# Electromagnets & the Electric Motor

Oersted showed current makes a field. Engineer it — coil the wire, add
iron — and you get a magnet you can switch, strengthen and reverse.
Put that magnet's field to work on another current, and you get motion:
the motor.

## The electromagnet

A single loop concentrates its field at the center. Stack $n$ loops per
metre into a **solenoid** and the fields add:

$$
B = \mu_0 n I
$$

Slide in an iron core and the atomic dipoles of iron line up with the
coil's field, multiplying it hundreds of times. Scrapyard cranes lift a
tonne of steel with current on — and drop it with current off. That
switchability is the whole point.

## Force on a current

A current is moving charge, and a magnetic field pushes moving charge
(the Lorentz force). For a wire of length $L$ perpendicular to the
field:

$$
F = B I L
$$

Direction: right hand again — fingers along I, curl toward B, thumb
pushes. Reverse *either* the current or the field and the force flips;
reverse both and it stays.

## The DC motor

Put a coil between magnet poles. The two sides carry current in
opposite directions, so the forces $BIL$ push them oppositely — a
**torque**:

$$
\tau = N B I A \sin\theta
$$

Problem: past the vertical, $\sin\theta$ changes sign and the torque
would push the coil *back*. The fix is the **commutator** — a split ring
that flips the current every half-turn, so the torque always pushes
forward. Watch it work — and watch it fail:

**Experiments to try**

1. **Normal run**: the coil spins up, then settles. Watch the *Speed &
   current* graph: as speed rises, the coil acts as a generator — its
   **back-EMF** opposes the battery and the current falls. A motor
   self-regulates.
2. **No commutator!**: the coil swings, overshoots, swings back — it
   oscillates around vertical and stalls. Now you know exactly what
   that split ring is for.
3. **Reverse polarity**: same motor, opposite spin. (Reverse the field
   instead — same result. Both — no change!)
4. **Heavy load**: speed drops, average current rises. Why does a
   jammed motor burn out? (Stall → no back-EMF → full $V/R$ current.)
5. **50 turns**: more torque, faster spin-up — $\tau \propto N$.

<Exercise title="Why motors gulp current at startup">
A motor with coil resistance 2 Ω runs on 12 V. What is the current the
instant it starts, and why is it much larger than the running current?

<Solution>
At start $\omega = 0$ → back-EMF = 0 → $I = V/R = 6$ A. Once spinning,
back-EMF subtracts from V, so current drops to whatever the load
demands. That inrush is why lights dim when a fridge compressor kicks
in — and why big motors need soft starters.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Torque budget">
You need double the torque at the same current and field. Give two
different design changes that achieve it.

<Solution>
$\tau = NBIA\sin\theta$: double the turns $N$, **or** double the coil
area $A$. (Doubling B or I would also work but violates the
constraint.) More turns costs wire and resistance; more area costs
space — real motor design is exactly this trade-off.
</Solution>
</Exercise>
