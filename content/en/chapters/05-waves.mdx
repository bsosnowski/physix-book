# Waves

A wave is a disturbance that travels without transporting matter. The
single most important fact about waves is the **superposition
principle**: when two waves overlap, the displacements simply add.

## Superposition

Send two identical waves in opposite directions along a string:

$$
y_1 = \tfrac{A}{2}\sin(kx - \omega t), \qquad
y_2 = \tfrac{A}{2}\sin(kx + \omega t)
$$

Their sum is a **standing wave**:

$$
y = y_1 + y_2 = A \sin(kx)\,\cos(\omega t)
$$

The pattern no longer travels — every point oscillates in place with
amplitude $A\sin(kx)$. Points where $\sin(kx) = 0$ are **nodes** and
never move at all.

## Standing waves

A string of length $L$ fixed at both ends can only host waves with nodes
at the ends, so $L = n\,\lambda/2$. The allowed frequencies are

$$
\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \qquad
f_n = \frac{v}{\lambda_n} = n\,\frac{v}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

This is why a guitar string sounds a fundamental plus overtones — and
why tightening it (larger $v = \sqrt{T/\rho}$) raises the pitch.

## Interference

Two coherent point sources emit identical circular waves. At any point,
the **path difference** $\Delta r = |r_1 - r_2|$ decides the outcome:

$$
\Delta r = m\lambda \;\Rightarrow\; \text{constructive}, \qquad
\Delta r = \left(m + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda \;\Rightarrow\; \text{destructive}
$$

The bright bands below are the loci of constant $\Delta r$ — hyperbolas
with the sources as foci. Their number is set by
<M v="d">d</M>/<M v="lambda">\lambda</M>: the largest possible path
difference is $d$ itself, so orders run up to $m_{max} = \lfloor d/\lambda \rfloor$.

**Experiments to try**

1. The vertical center line is always bright. Why? (What is $\Delta r$
   there, for *any* settings?)
2. Shrink <M v="lambda">\lambda</M> with the sources fixed: fringes
   multiply. Check the *Max order* readout against $\lfloor d/\lambda \rfloor$.
3. Try the **d = λ** preset: only the central maximum and the $m = \pm1$
   directions survive. What happens to the pattern as $d < \lambda$?
4. The probe sits on the center line — its intensity is always 1.
   Predict what it would read halfway between two bright bands.

> This is the geometry behind Young's double slit, antenna arrays, and
> noise-cancelling headphones — same equation, different waves.
