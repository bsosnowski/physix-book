# Reflection & Mirrors

Every surface you see, you see by reflected light. The rule is almost
insultingly simple — and it builds telescopes.

## The law of reflection

$$
\theta_{incident} = \theta_{reflected}
$$

Both measured from the **normal** (perpendicular to the surface). On a
smooth surface, parallel rays stay parallel — *specular* reflection, a
mirror. On a rough one they scatter — *diffuse* reflection, which is why
you can read this page from any angle but see your face only in glass.

## Plane mirrors

A flat mirror forms a **virtual** image: same size, upright, as far
behind the mirror as you are in front ($v = -u$). No light actually goes
behind the mirror — your brain extends the diverging rays backwards.
The image is also *front–back* reversed, which we mislabel "left–right".

## Spherical mirrors

Curve the mirror and rays gain a meeting point. For a spherical mirror
of radius $R$, paraxial rays focus at

$$
f = \frac{R}{2}, \qquad \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}
$$

- **Concave** (caved-in, $f>0$): can form **real** images — light truly
  converges and can be caught on a screen. Object inside $f$ → virtual,
  magnified (shaving/makeup mirrors).
- **Convex** (bulging, $f<0$): always virtual, upright, diminished —
  a wide-angle view. Hence car side mirrors and shop security mirrors.

**Experiments to try**

1. **Plane mirror**: drag the object — the image mirrors it perfectly,
   always the same size. Check $m = 1$ in the readout.
2. **Concave, far object**: real, inverted, small image near F. Slide
   the object toward F and watch the image race away and grow.
3. **Shaving mirror**: object inside f — upright and magnified. Move
   your face (the object) slowly outward past F: the image flips!
4. **Car side mirror**: whatever you do, the image stays small and
   upright. Why does that make objects "farther than they appear"?

<Exercise title="The full-length mirror">
How tall must a plane mirror be for you to see your whole body — and
does walking backwards help?

<Solution>
Half your height, mounted with its top midway between your eyes and the
top of your head. Ray geometry: each ray reflects at the midpoint
between eye and body-point, so the needed mirror spans exactly half
your height — *independent of distance*. Walking backwards changes
nothing. (Try it with the object arrow: the rays always hit the mirror
at half-heights.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Solar cooker">
A concave mirror of radius 80 cm aims at the Sun. Where should the pot
go, and why does the image of the Sun form there?

<Solution>
$f = R/2 = 40$ cm from the mirror. The Sun is effectively at infinity
($u \to \infty$), so $1/v = 1/f$: its image — a tiny, intensely bright
disk — forms at the focal point. Put the pot there. Same principle as
a reflecting telescope, run in reverse.
</Solution>
</Exercise>
