# Sound

Sound is a wave you can feel in your chest. Unlike the string waves of
chapter 5, air cannot carry shear — so sound is **longitudinal**: the
air molecules oscillate *along* the direction of travel, creating
compressions and rarefactions.

## What sound is

A speaker cone pushes and pulls; pressure ripples propagate at
≈ 343 m/s in room-temperature air (faster in water ≈ 1480 m/s, steel
≈ 5000 m/s — stiffer media transmit faster). The universal wave relation
holds:

$$
v = \lambda f
$$

- **Frequency ↔ pitch**: 300 Hz is a low hum, 800 Hz a clear tone.
- **Amplitude ↔ loudness**: measured logarithmically in decibels;
  halving amplitude drops about 6 dB.

## Beats

Two nearby frequencies superpose into a tone that *throbs*:

$$
\sin(2\pi f_1 t) + \sin(2\pi f_2 t)
= 2\cos\!\left(2\pi \tfrac{f_1-f_2}{2} t\right)\sin\!\left(2\pi \tfrac{f_1+f_2}{2} t\right)
$$

The envelope pulses at the **beat frequency** $|f_1 - f_2|$. Piano
tuners listen for the throbbing to slow to zero. Try it with your ears:

<Tone f1={300} f2={340} />

Set f₂ a few Hz from f₁ and count the slow wah-wah-wah. Now set them
equal — one steady tone. That null is how instruments get tuned.

**Experiments to try**

1. **Beats** preset in the simulation: the probe graph shows the
   envelope; the readout says 40 Hz. (The animation is slowed so your
   eye can follow — your ears above hear the real frequencies.)
2. **Octave**: 200 + 400 Hz — no throbbing, a consonant blend. Beats
   need *close* frequencies.
3. Halve the amplitude: the loudness readout drops ≈ 6 dB, the pattern
   is unchanged.

## The Doppler effect

A moving source chases its own wavefronts: ahead of it they bunch
(shorter λ, higher pitch), behind they stretch:

$$
f_{ahead} = \frac{f_0}{1 - \beta}, \qquad
f_{behind} = \frac{f_0}{1 + \beta}, \qquad \beta = \frac{v_{source}}{v_{sound}}
$$

<Sim id="sound" params={{ f1: 400, doppler: 0.5 }} />

**Experiments:** raise <M v="doppler">\beta</M> toward 0.8 — the heard-
ahead frequency explodes toward infinity as the source approaches the
wave speed. At $\beta = 1$ the fronts pile into a single shock: the
sonic boom. Ambulances, race cars and redshifted galaxies all run this
same equation.

<Exercise title="Tuning by beats">
A tuning fork at 440 Hz sounds against a piano string; you hear 3 beats
per second. Is the string at 437 or 443 Hz? How can one small
adjustment tell you which?

<Solution>
Either — beats give $|f - 440| = 3$. Tighten the string slightly
(raises f): if beats *slow*, you were at 437 moving toward 440; if they
*speed up*, you were at 443 moving away. The direction of change
disambiguates.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Ambulance arithmetic">
A siren at 800 Hz approaches at 30 m/s (sound: 343 m/s). What frequency
do you hear before and after it passes? What is the total drop?

<Solution>
Approaching: $800/(1 - 30/343) \approx 879$ Hz. Receding:
$800/(1 + 30/343) \approx 736$ Hz. The famous *nee-naw* drop is ≈ 143 Hz
— about three semitones, right in the ear's most sensitive range.
</Solution>
</Exercise>
